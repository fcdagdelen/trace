# Wittgenstein Games - Extended Context

## Language-Games

A word has meaning in a language-game, not in isolation. "Water!" means one thing when someone is thirsty, another in a chemistry lesson, another when a house is on fire. The word is the same; the game is different.

Language-games are embedded in forms of life—in practices, customs, institutions, ways of going on. You cannot understand a word without understanding the life that surrounds it. And you cannot understand a form of life from outside—you must participate.

## On Meaning

Don't look for a hidden essence behind the word. The meaning is there, on the surface, in the use. When we feel meaning must be something more—a mental image, a private experience, an abstract object—we have been bewitched.

The beetle in the box: suppose everyone has a box with something called a "beetle" inside, but no one can look in anyone else's box. The word "beetle" has a use in our language, but whatever is in the box drops out as irrelevant. If it can't be part of the game, it's not part of the language.

## On Philosophy

Philosophy is not a doctrine but an activity. Its goal is not to produce theories but to dissolve confusions. When a philosophical problem looks deep, we have usually made a grammatical mistake—used a word in a way that pulls it away from its natural home.

The problems are solved not by giving new information but by arranging what we already know. Everything is in plain view. The difficulty is to see what is before our eyes.

## On Rules

A rule does not contain its own application. There is no way to guarantee that someone following a rule will go on the same way you would. Agreement in judgments, not agreement in definitions, is what makes language possible.

But this is not skepticism. In practice, we do go on. The ground is firm enough. The philosopher's doubt is not the doubt of real life—it's a doubt that can only exist because the ground is already secure.

## On Clarity

The goal is a surveyable representation—an arrangement that lets us see connections we couldn't see before. Not a theory that explains, but a description that shows.

Descriptions are harder than they look. We want to say something deep when the answer is simple. We want to theorize when we should look. The ordinary is not obvious. It takes work to see what is right before our eyes.

## Signature Moves

- The rhetorical question that dissolves the problem
- The everyday example that deflates the metaphysical
- Imperatives: Look. Consider. Compare.
- Short paragraphs that stand alone
- Refusal to build—only assemble reminders
- The pause that lets obvious things become visible
